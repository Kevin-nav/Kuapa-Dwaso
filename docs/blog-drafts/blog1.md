# Listening Before Building: What Farmers and Traders in Tarkwa Taught Us

Kuapa Dwaso means Farmer's Market in Akan. The idea behind it is simple. Farmers should be able to find reliable buyers, and buyers should be able to count on dependable produce.

But a simple idea is not the same as a correct one. Before writing a single line of code, we wanted to hear from the people who live this problem every day. So in July 2026, our team members Ms Catherine Bruce Vochere and Ms Hannah Nankon Gyekyiwaa travelled to Tarkwa, a busy mining town in the Tarkwa-Nsuaem Municipality of Ghana's Western Region. They visited the market near the University of Mines and Technology (UMaT), just south of the town centre. Twice.

On Wednesday, 1st July, they spent time with traders in the market, asking how they source produce, what frustrates them, and what happens to food before it ever reaches their stalls. On Friday, 3rd July, one of Tarkwa's official market days, they returned to meet farmers who had travelled in to sell their harvest.

Those two days changed how we think about the problem we are solving.

## The long road to market

The first thing traders wanted to talk about was transport, and once they explained it, we understood why.

Produce rarely travels straight from a farm to a market like the one in Tarkwa. Many farms sit far from good roads, so the harvest often has to be carried by hand to a spot a tricycle can reach. The tricycle takes it to another point where it can finally be loaded onto a truck for the longer journey into town.

So a single trip can look like this:

> Farm or village, then manual carrying, then a tricycle, then a truck, then the city market.

Every stage costs money. Every stage is also a chance for delay, rough handling, or spoilage. By the time a tomato reaches a stall in Tarkwa, it has already paid its way through several hands, and those costs show up in the final price.

Something else surprised us. Many of the traders we met do not buy from farmers at all. They buy from other traders and middlemen. That extra distance between the person who grows the food and the person who sells it makes life harder for both sides. Traders struggle to get a predictable supply, and farmers struggle to see where real, dependable demand actually is.

## The farmer arrives already exposed

The Friday visit showed us the same supply chain from the other end, and honestly, it was the more sobering conversation.

Picture a farmer's week. They harvest, pay for every stage of that transport journey we just described, and arrive at the market with their produce. And then, quite often, they discover they have no confirmed buyer waiting for them.

At that point the money is already spent. If the produce is perishable, the clock is ticking. There is very little room to negotiate.

Farmers told us that some traders do not have cash on hand when the produce arrives. So the farmer faces a hard choice. Take the produce back home, which rarely makes financial sense, or hand it over to a trader and hope to be paid later. Most hand it over.

That is a weak position to bargain from, and it often ends in low prices or informal credit arrangements the farmer never really chose.

This was the moment our understanding shifted. The problem is not simply that farmers and buyers cannot find each other. The deeper problem is that produce starts moving before anyone has agreed on anything. There is no dependable process before the journey begins, so all the risk lands on the farmer.

## Both sides need someone they can count on

It would be easy to frame this as farmers versus traders, but that is not what we saw. Both sides are dealing with the same underlying issue: unreliability.

Buyers need to know the quantity will show up, the quality will match what was agreed, and delivery will happen on time. Farmers need to know the demand is real, the payment terms are clear, and their produce is not travelling somewhere without a genuine path to a sale.

This is now the centre of how we are building Kuapa Dwaso. Our approach starts with the buyer. Confirm what buyers actually need, find farmers who can meet that need, agree on quality and payment terms upfront, gather quantities that make transport worthwhile, and then coordinate delivery.

In other words, produce should move because a sale exists, not in the hope that one might.

## The assumption we got wrong

The Friday conversations also corrected us on something we thought we knew.

Going in, we assumed most farmers would not have smartphones, so we planned for USSD or SMS commands as the main way to use Kuapa Dwaso. We were wrong, at least for Tarkwa. Many of the farmers we spoke to either own a smartphone or live with someone who does and can help them use one.

That does not mean every farmer in every community has reliable smartphone access. But it does mean USSD does not need to be our starting point.

So Kuapa Dwaso will launch with a lightweight, smartphone first experience. A farmer can sign up directly, or with help from a family member or a Kuapa Dwaso representative. SMS will still play a role, sending simple updates about orders, produce status, and payments, so farmers do not need to stay online.

We will keep testing this assumption as we enter new communities. If the evidence shows a real need for USSD or another offline channel later, we will build it based on how people actually use the platform, not on what we imagined in a planning meeting.

## Problems that start before the harvest

Farmers also spoke about the rising cost of inputs like fertiliser. It was a useful reminder that their challenges begin long before produce reaches any market.

We will be honest here. Kuapa Dwaso cannot solve everything at once, and input financing and credit sit outside our initial scope. But these conversations point to future possibilities, such as partnerships with input suppliers or coordinated bulk purchasing, once we have built a trusted farmer network.

For now, our focus stays narrow: making the relationship between farmers and buyers dependable.

## What Tarkwa changed for us

Two days in the field gave us more direction than months of planning. Here is what we are taking forward:

- Farmers should not move produce until credible demand exists.
- Transport must be coordinated in quantities that make commercial sense.
- Payment terms must be clear to farmers before they agree to anything.
- Quality expectations should be settled before produce is collected.
- Farmers keep the final say on whether to accept an offer.
- Buyers need verified, dependable supply rather than hopeful listings.
- A smartphone first launch is practical, with assisted access and SMS updates where they help.
- We keep learning from real transactions, not assumptions.

One day, Kuapa Dwaso may include storage and warehouse infrastructure that gives farmers a buffer when an immediate sale is not favourable. But we will not start by building warehouses. Our first model will be asset light, led by demand, and supported by partnerships.

## Still listening

These visits did not answer every question. What they did was hand us better questions.

Which crops have dependable demand? What quantity makes a transport trip worth the cost? Which buyers pay reliably? What quality standards decide whether produce is accepted or rejected? Should payment periods differ from crop to crop?

These questions are now guiding our pilot and our next field visits.

Kuapa Dwaso is being built from the experiences of farmers, traders, buyers, and agricultural partners, not from what we imagine the market needs. By listening first and improving through real transactions, we hope to build a Farmer's Market worthy of the name: one that gives farmers real access, real choices, and a dependable route to market.
