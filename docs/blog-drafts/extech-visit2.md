# What We Learned From Our Visit to Extech Agricultural Services

On Friday, 7 August 2026, two of us from Kuapa Dwaso, Ms Catherine Bruce Vuochere and Mr Kevin Amisom Nchorbuno, spent the day with the team at Extech Agricultural Services in Takoradi.

We went there with one goal. We wanted to learn from people who are already doing the work we are preparing to do. Kuapa Dwaso is getting ready for its first pilot, and there is only so much you can learn from a desk. Some lessons only come from sitting with people who have spent years in the market.

The Extech team welcomed us warmly, answered our questions honestly, and even took us to see a warehouse so we could understand the physical side of agricultural storage and trade. But the biggest lessons of the day were not about the building. They were about relationships, demand, and knowing where to start.

## Start with the market, not the building

Before this visit, a warehouse felt like a natural first step for a business like ours. Store the produce, then find the buyers. Simple, right?

The visit taught us why that thinking is backwards.

A warehouse is expensive to build and expensive to run. It needs staff, security, electricity, and maintenance whether it is full or empty. And here is the hard truth we sat with on the ride home: a warehouse does not create demand. If produce goes into storage without reliable buyers waiting for it, you are simply paying to watch food lose value.

So we made a decision. Kuapa Dwaso will not begin by building or operating its own warehouse. Instead, we will focus on the thing that actually makes trade work: connecting real buyer demand with verified farmer supply, and coordinating the movement of produce between them. Where storage is needed, we will use existing facilities and partners.

Owning our own warehouse is still part of the long-term plan, roughly three years out. But that decision will be earned through evidence, meaning repeated transactions, real storage demand, and the numbers to run a facility sustainably.

## Know the buyer before the produce moves

Another lesson that stuck with us: demand comes first, always.

Before a single sack of produce is collected, we need clear answers from the buyer:

- What crop and variety do they want?
- How much?
- To what quality standard?
- Delivered where, and by when?
- At what price?
- And how quickly will they pay?

Only when those answers are clear do we go to the farmers, confirm who can supply the order, agree the terms, and coordinate quality checks, aggregation, and transport.

This protects everyone. Farmers do not transport produce with no sure route to sale. Buyers get confidence that what was promised will actually arrive. And to be clear about our role: farmers keep ownership of their produce until they accept a sale. We do not buy everything upfront and gamble on finding buyers later. We coordinate, we make terms transparent, and we make sure the deal is fulfilled properly.

## Why we are not starting alone

Agriculture is not a sector you walk into cold. The people already working in it hold years of market knowledge, farmer relationships, and buyer connections. They have also survived the problems you cannot see from the outside.

The advice from the Extech team was practical: lean on experienced partners while you learn, do real transactions, and build your own capacity step by step.

We were glad the conversation went further than advice. We reached an early understanding that Extech Agricultural Services is willing to support Kuapa Dwaso in its first stages. That support could include connecting us with farmers in the areas we plan to work, helping us fulfil small controlled pilot orders, and opening a path to larger opportunities for crops like maize.

For a young business, this matters enormously. Instead of building an entire agricultural network from nothing, we get to learn alongside people who already know the terrain. The exact shape of the collaboration will be defined as we prepare for operations, with crops, quantities, quality, pricing, and payment terms agreed for each transaction.

## Choosing our first crop carefully

Maize came up often in our discussions. It trades in large volumes and connects to established buyers and processors, which makes it a strong candidate for our first pilot. But we are not choosing a crop just because it is common or easy to store.

Our first crop must be backed by evidence on both sides of the trade. Confirmed buyer demand. Available farmer supply. Sensible harvest timing, manageable losses, affordable transport, dependable payment behaviour, and a margin that survives after all the costs of fulfilment.

The potential partnership with Extech gives us a way to test all of this through a small, controlled transaction before we commit to anything bigger.

## Partnerships are the strategy, not a shortcut

If there was one big idea running through the whole day, it was this: we do not need to own every part of the value chain to improve how it works.

Farmers, buyers, transporters, processors, and warehouse operators each already hold a piece of what it takes to move produce successfully. Our value is in coordinating those pieces around verified demand, clear quality expectations, transparent payment terms, and accountable delivery.

In our early stages, partnerships will help us reach and verify farmers, understand production capacity and harvest periods, access credible buyers, coordinate transport, and use storage only when a transaction actually requires it.

Our goal is to learn responsibly, build a track record of reliable transactions, and grow into independence rather than pretending we already have it.

## Where we go from here

The visit gave us a clear sequence for what comes next:

> Validate demand, identify the right crops, verify farmers, agree quality and payment terms, coordinate logistics, complete a controlled pilot, measure the results, then repeat what works.

Our immediate objective is simple to state and hard to do: prove that Kuapa Dwaso can reliably move produce from farmers to confirmed buyers while creating value for everyone in between.

We will measure everything. Quantities sourced and accepted, transport and handling costs, delivery times, payment periods, losses, farmer experience, buyer experience, and the margin left at the end. Those numbers will guide our decisions far better than any projection, including the eventual question of whether a warehouse of our own is truly justified.

## A closing thought

Earlier conversations with farmers and traders in Tarkwa showed us the problem clearly. Farmers need dependable buyers. Buyers need dependable supply. And fragmented transport adds cost and risk for everyone in between.

Our day in Takoradi showed us a practical way to begin solving it, through confirmed demand, small controlled pilots, and partnerships with people who already know this work.

We are grateful to the team at Extech Agricultural Services for their openness and their willingness to explore this early collaboration. As we move toward our first pilot, we will keep doing what this visit taught us to do: listen first, test small, and let the evidence lead.
