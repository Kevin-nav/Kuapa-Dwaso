# Listening Before Building: What Farmers and Traders in Tarkwa Taught Us

Kuapa Dwaso means “Farmer’s Market” in Akan. From the beginning, our goal has been to help farmers find reliable buyers while helping buyers obtain dependable agricultural produce.

However, we knew that building a useful product required more than assumptions. We needed to speak with the people who experience these problems directly.

During our first market engagement in Tarkwa, we spoke with traders about how they obtain produce, the difficulties they face and the journey food takes before reaching the market. We later returned on a Friday morning to speak with farmers who had travelled to sell their produce.

Together, these conversations changed how we understood the problem.

## A long and expensive journey to market

One of the strongest concerns raised by traders was transportation.

Produce does not always move directly from a farm to a city market. Poor road access means it may first need to be carried from a farm or village to a point accessible by a tricycle. The tricycle then transports it to another location where it can be loaded onto a truck for the longer journey.

A single journey can therefore look like this:

> Farm or village → manual loading → tricycle → truck → city market

Every stage introduces another cost. It also creates another opportunity for delays, handling damage and produce loss.

Traders told us that these transportation expenses ultimately affect the price at which food can be sold. We also learned that many of the traders we engaged in Tarkwa did not purchase directly from farmers. Instead, they bought through other traders or intermediaries, creating additional separation between the people producing the food and those selling it to consumers.

This fragmentation makes it difficult for traders to obtain predictable supply and equally difficult for farmers to understand where dependable demand exists.

## Farmers carry the risk before finding a buyer

The Friday visit helped us understand the same supply chain from the farmers’ perspective.

Farmers may harvest their produce, pay to move it through several transportation stages and arrive at the market without a confirmed buyer. By this point, they have already invested considerable money and effort in reaching the city.

If the produce is perishable, the farmer has limited time to negotiate.

We heard that some traders may not have funds available immediately. A farmer who has already transported produce to the market may consequently feel pressured to leave it with a trader and accept payment later.

This places the farmer in a vulnerable position. The transportation cost has already been paid, the produce may begin deteriorating, and taking it back may not be economically practical. These conditions weaken the farmer’s bargaining power and can result in unfavourable prices or informal credit arrangements.

We realised that the problem was not simply that farmers and buyers could not find one another. The deeper problem was the absence of a dependable, coordinated process before produce began moving.

## Reliability is needed on both sides

Farmers need buyers they can depend on, but traders and other buyers also need reliable sellers.

Buyers need confidence that the expected quantity will be available, that the produce will meet the agreed quality and that delivery will happen when promised. Farmers need confidence that demand is genuine, that payment terms are clear and that their produce will not be moved without a realistic path to sale.

This insight now sits at the centre of Kuapa Dwaso.

Our starting approach is to confirm buyer requirements first, identify farmers who can meet those requirements, agree on quality and payment terms, aggregate commercially viable quantities and coordinate delivery.

Rather than asking farmers to transport produce blindly and search for buyers afterwards, we want movement to be supported by confirmed demand.

## Questioning our assumptions about technology

The Friday conversations also challenged one of our early assumptions.

We initially believed that a USSD or command-based SMS service might be required as the primary way for farmers to join and use Kuapa Dwaso. We assumed smartphone access would be a major barrier.

The farmers we engaged told us that many either had smartphones themselves or had someone within their household who could help them access one. This does not mean every farmer in every community has dependable smartphone access. It does, however, mean that USSD does not need to be the primary onboarding channel for our initial pilot.

Kuapa Dwaso will therefore begin with a lightweight, smartphone-first experience. A farmer can create an account directly or receive assistance from a trusted household member or Kuapa Dwaso representative.

SMS can still serve an important role by delivering simple notifications about orders, produce status and payments without requiring farmers to remain online continuously.

As we enter new communities, we will continue testing this assumption. If the evidence later demonstrates a meaningful need for USSD or another offline channel, we can introduce it based on actual usage conditions.

## Other challenges extend beyond the sale

Farmers also raised the cost of agricultural inputs, including fertiliser. This reminded us that the challenges farmers experience begin long before produce reaches the market.

Kuapa Dwaso will not attempt to solve every agricultural problem at once. Input financing and credit are outside our initial scope. However, these conversations point towards future opportunities, including partnerships with input suppliers or coordinated bulk purchasing once we have established a trusted farmer network.

For now, our priority is narrower: improving the reliability of the relationship between farmers and buyers.

## What these visits changed for Kuapa Dwaso

The visits gave us several important lessons:

- Farmers should not be required to move produce before credible demand is established.
- Transportation must be coordinated in commercially viable quantities.
- Buyer payment terms must be disclosed to farmers before they agree to a transaction.
- Quality expectations should be confirmed before produce is collected.
- Farmers should retain control over whether to accept a buyer’s offer.
- Buyers need verified and dependable supply rather than uncertain listings.
- Smartphone-first onboarding is practical for our initial pilot, with assisted access and SMS notifications where appropriate.
- Kuapa Dwaso must continue learning from real transactions and field relationships.

Our long-term vision may include storage and warehouse infrastructure that gives farmers an additional buffer when immediate sale is not favourable. However, we will not begin by constructing or operating our own warehouses. Our initial model will be asset-light, demand-led and supported by partnerships.

## Continuing to listen

These visits did not answer every question. They helped us identify the right questions to ask.

Which crops have dependable demand? What quantity makes transportation commercially sustainable? Which buyers pay reliably? What quality standards cause produce to be accepted or rejected? How should payment periods differ between crops?

We are now using these questions to guide our pilot and future field engagements.

Kuapa Dwaso is being built from the experiences of farmers, traders, buyers and agricultural partners—not only from what we imagine the market needs. By listening first, testing carefully and improving through real transactions, we hope to build a Farmer’s Market that gives farmers greater access, stronger choices and more dependable routes to market.
